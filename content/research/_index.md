I work along two lines, both concerned with the conditions of aesthetic perception. The first is empirical and art-historical (§1). The second is philosophical (§2).

## 1. Art perception: history, theory, experiment

Paintings are looked at, but the looking is not uniform. One source of variation is historical: different periods built different viewing structures into their pictures. I work on the painting-beholder relationship in eighteenth-century French landscape painting, where the interplay between immersion and dramatic address remains unresolved in Fried's (1980) influential account. Another source of variation is cultural. Do viewers from different backgrounds fixate the same regions of a painting? My doctoral project tests this with eye-tracking, taking Baxandall's (1972) "period eye" as its starting point. A separate strand asks how claims about culture and perception have travelled between art history and psychology, and what was lost or distorted in transit.

## 2. Aesthetics and the philosophy of art

Philosophers of art widely agree that seeing a work for yourself puts you in a different position from hearing it described. What that difference consists in remains open. I work on the conditions that aesthetic experience must satisfy: what acquaintance with a work requires, and what imagination must do to sustain it. A separate line of work concerns the boundaries of art. New practices are regularly admitted into the category, but the admission raises questions that definitions of art tend to leave unanswered: is the classificatory act a discovery or a decision, and does it matter when it happens?
