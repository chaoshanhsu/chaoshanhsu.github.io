My research asks a single question across two registers: **what are the normative conditions for genuinely perceiving a work of art, and how are those conditions culturally realised?**

The philosophical register yields theories of aesthetic acquaintance, aesthetic imagination, and art-kind membership. The empirical register yields cross-cultural eye-tracking studies that test whether cultural training systematically reshapes visual engagement with art. The two halves meet in the concept of the *period eye*—Michael Baxandall's idea that historically and culturally specific skills shape artistic perception—which I both theorise philosophically and operationalise experimentally.

---

## Normativity in Aesthetic Experience

What must hold for someone to count as genuinely perceiving, imagining, or engaging with a work of art? I develop the **Corrective Feedback Criterion (CFC)** and the **Configuration-Response Link (CRL)** as frameworks for answering this question. The CFC specifies a corrective structure that aesthetic mental imagery must possess; the CRL connects perceptual configurations to appropriate aesthetic responses. Together they ground accounts of aesthetic acquaintance, aesthetic imagination, and the limits of AI-generated art.

## Aesthetic Perception and Its Cultural Conditions

If aesthetic perception has normative conditions, does cultural training change how people meet those conditions? My dissertation, "Culture Writes, Eye Decodes," anchors this question in Baxandall's *period eye*, which I operationalise as a domain-specifically distributed categorial profile. Using eye-tracking, I compare gaze behaviour across participants schooled in different art-historical traditions (Austrian, Japanese, Korean, Mandarin) to test whether culture systematically shapes the visual encounter with paintings.

*Related papers:* [Cultural Differences in Gaze Patterns over Paintings and Photographs](/5/) · Dissertation (in progress)

## Art Ontology and Art-Kind Membership

When does a new practice become an art kind? I argue that pioneering artworks do not belong to the kind they inaugurate at the moment of their creation; kind-membership requires the stabilisation of a practice. This work draws on a buck-passing account of art-kind properties.

*Related papers:* [When Do Pioneers Become Art?](/2/)
