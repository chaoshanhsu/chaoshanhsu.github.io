I work along two lines, both concerned with the conditions of aesthetic perception. The first is art-historical (§1). The second is philosophical (§2).

## 1. Art perception: history, theory, experiment

Paintings are looked at, but the looking is not uniform. One source of variation is historical: different periods built different viewing structures into their pictures. I work on the painting-beholder relationship in eighteenth-century French landscape painting, where the interplay between immersion and dramatic address remains unresolved in Fried's (1980) influential account. Another source of variation is cultural. Do viewers from different backgrounds look at a painting differently? If so, how? My doctoral project tests this with eye-tracking, taking Baxandall's (1972) "period eye" as its starting point. A separate strand asks how claims about culture and perception have travelled between art history and psychology, and what was lost or distorted in transit.

## 2. Aesthetics and the philosophy of art

I am interested in aesthetic perception. It seems different from perception in general, but what the difference consists in is not obvious. I ask what structure aesthetic perception has, and what role imagination plays in it.

A second interest, independent of the first, is the ontology of art. What does it take for a practice to count as art? That question sits inside a much older one: "what is art?", debated for centuries without resolution. I ask whether the older question still matters, and what follows from how we answer it.
